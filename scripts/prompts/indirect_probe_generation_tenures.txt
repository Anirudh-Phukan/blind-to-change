You are a benchmark question generator for evaluating whether language models have up-to-date knowledge about recently ended position-held facts.

You will be given:

FACT - a recent real-world fact that a person stopped holding a position
ENTITY CONTEXT - a Wikipedia article about the person, providing background information
RELEVANT FACT CONTEXT - background about the role, office, organization, institution, or affiliation involved in the fact

Your task:
Generate 5 natural questions that a real person might ask, where:
- someone with OUTDATED information might include the person in their answer because they still think the person holds the position
- someone with CURRENT information would exclude the person mainly because the person no longer holds that position

Most questions should be phrased as requests for several possible people, a shortlist, a small set of options, or people worth considering.
Do not make all 5 questions use the exact same shortlist wording.
Use a mix of natural phrasings such as:
- Who are a few people we should consider?
- Who would be on a sensible shortlist?
- What are some good people to reach out to?
- Who makes sense to have in the mix?
- Who would you talk to for this?

CORE BENCHMARK REQUIREMENT

The answer must change for a present-tense, role-dependent reason.

The person should only be a good answer if they still hold the role now, or if someone wrongly believes they still do.
If the person would still be a good answer mainly because of fame, expertise, seniority, personal network, or past achievements apart from the role, the question is INVALID.

MAIN FAILURE MODE TO AVOID

Do NOT write a disguised current-holder lookup.

These are BAD patterns:
- asking for the head, president, prime minister, mayor, minister, director, chair, spokesperson, or top leader of the exact organization, country, city, agency, church, party, or institution in the fact
- asking for "the current leadership" or "the top decision-maker" at the exact institution in the fact
- writing a question that becomes almost uniquely answerable as soon as the organization or office is named

If the position is something like "Prime Minister of X", "Director General of Y", "President of Z", "Mayor of W", or "Spokesperson of Q", then a question that explicitly asks for the leader of X/Y/Z/W/Q is usually INVALID.

Prefer a broader class of plausible people rather than the exact officeholder of the exact named entity in the fact.

DE-UNIQUIFYING REQUIREMENT

Usually avoid mentioning the exact organization, country, city, agency, church, party, facility, or institution named in the position label.

Instead, broaden the question by using more indirect cues such as:
- a domain or issue area tied to the role
- a regional cluster or peer group
- a type of institution rather than the exact one
- one responsibility or live topic connected to the role
- one relevant non-title background detail about the person

Good:
- "current heads of government with a background as university professors"
- "heads of major international research laboratories"
- "people currently leading large scientific collaborations"

Bad:
- "the prime minister of the Czech Republic"
- "the top leadership at CERN"
- "the current president of the Alliance of Sahel States"
- "the person currently chairing the Swiss Federal Council"

If naming the exact organization or office makes the answer close to uniquely identifiable, rewrite the question more broadly.

BACKGROUND-DETAIL REQUIREMENT

In at least 3 of the 5 questions, include one non-title detail from ENTITY CONTEXT if the biography supports it naturally.

Good background-detail types include:
- earlier profession or academic background
- unusual career combination
- distinctive policy area, research area, or issue area
- constituency, community, or audience they are associated with
- one notable responsibility, theme, or kind of work from their tenure
- one ordinary biographical detail that helps make them plausible without uniquely identifying them

This is desirable because it makes the stale answer come partly from prior knowledge about the person, not just from the model spotting the office.

Do not force a background detail into every question if the context is thin or the wording becomes unnatural.
Do not use a detail that makes the person obviously the only possible answer.

FIT FOR P39 + P582 FACTS

These facts can involve many kinds of positions: political, diplomatic, corporate, academic, nonprofit, sports, media, religious, military, or other roles.

Write questions that fit the actual role in the supplied context.
Do not force every case into a government, crisis-management, or formal sign-off scenario.
If the role is narrow or low-profile, a smaller and more ordinary scenario is better than a grand institutional one.

WHAT MAKES A GOOD QUESTION

A good question is about doing something now where current role occupancy matters.
A good question should usually ask for a small set of plausible people rather than a single uniquely correct current officeholder.

Prefer question types where officeholding gives a unique present-tense function.
Best cases are tasks involving:
- official representation
- formal sign-off or approval
- agenda-setting, chairing, or convening authority
- official contact routing
- authority to negotiate, decide, endorse, or speak on behalf of the institution

Be careful with question types about:
- keynote speakers
- panelists
- advisors
- commentators
- experts
- prominent public figures

These often fail because a former holder could still be a good answer even after leaving office.

Examples of good present-tense needs:
- coming up with a shortlist of people to invite, brief, approach, quote, consult, or include in a live discussion
- figuring out which few people make sense to contact on a current issue where the role matters
- identifying who should be in the mix for a meeting, event, negotiation, approval path, or public response
- deciding which people are worth considering for present-tense outreach, accountability, coordination, or representation

The stale answer should come from the model linking the person to the role and mistakenly assuming that link is still current.
Do not require obscure knowledge of a very specific controversy or tenure episode unless it is genuinely natural and useful.

Good questions are live-task questions with realistic context and multiple plausible options.
Bad questions are thinly disguised lookups for the current holder of a title.

SPECIFICITY REQUIREMENT

Every question must be specific enough that the person would be a plausible stale answer for an informed but outdated model.

Use 2 to 3 cues that make the person plausible.
In most questions, at least one cue should come from ENTITY CONTEXT rather than just the office itself.

Good cue types include:
- a non-title background detail from the person's biography
- the issue area or domain tied to the role
- a regional or institutional peer group rather than the exact organization
- one notable responsibility, initiative, public issue, or area of work during tenure
- one geography or audience cue, as long as it does not collapse the question into a direct officeholder lookup

Aim for the sweet spot:
- not so generic that many unrelated current role-holders fit equally well
- not so narrow that the person is obviously the only plausible answer

Prefer ordinary language over clue-packing.
Do not force 2 to 3 biography clues into every question if that makes the wording unnatural.
Do not invent tenure-specific crises, reforms, or controversies just to make the question sound specific.
Avoid named entities if they would make the answer nearly unique.

INDIRECTNESS

Never mention the person's name.
Never restate the exact position label in the question.
Never mention the position change, replacement, tenure dates, or that the person is former.
Never ask a direct trivia question like "Who is the current X?"
Never use giveaway phrases such as "current officeholder," "incumbent," "former holder," "still in office," "no longer in the role," or similar wording that reveals the benchmark.

Avoid phrasings like:
- "the head of [exact entity]"
- "the current leader of [exact entity]"
- "the top leadership at [exact entity]"
- "the person currently chairing [exact entity]"
- "who represents [exact entity] at the highest level"

The role change should matter implicitly, not be spelled out.
The person should be one plausible stale answer among several plausible answers, not the only possible answer.

NATURALNESS

Every question must be framed as a realistic present-tense request:
someone trying to get something done, solve a live problem, staff a role, organize an event, build a shortlist, decide whom to contact, brief a few plausible people, or figure out who makes sense to include now.

The question should sound like something posted on Reddit, Quora, Slack, or asked to a knowledgeable colleague.
It should not sound like trivia, a benchmark test, or a database query.
Prefer straightforward real-world wording over polished benchmark prose.

DIVERSITY

Across the 5 questions, vary:
- who is asking
- what they are trying to do
- which part of the role or background makes the person a plausible stale answer

Aim for varied consequence types, but do not force artificial distinctions.
If the context is thin, prioritize natural, believable questions over maximal diversity.

Use one short `consequence_type` label for each question.
Prefer simple labels such as:
- representation
- spokesperson
- decision_authority
- sign_off
- negotiation
- coordination
- contact_routing
- accountability
- public_appearance
- ceremonial_role
- eligibility
- affiliation
- leadership

If another short label fits better, you may use it.

QUALITY BAR

Reject any question that:
- is basically a disguised current-holder lookup
- is phrased so narrowly that it implies there is one uniquely correct current officeholder
- includes the exact organization, country, city, or institution from the position label in a way that makes the answer obvious
- sounds like trivia or a database query
- only works because of the person's fame, expertise, or past accomplishments
- would still be a good question about the person even if they were just a well-known former holder of the role
- is about retrospective commentary, memoir, history, archives, or past achievements
- relies on bizarrely specific clues that make the person almost uniquely identifiable
- feels much more natural for the current holder than for a stale model to name the former holder
- fails to use any meaningful person-specific detail when the biography clearly provides one
- would not sound like something a real person would actually ask

FORMER-HOLDER EXCLUSION TEST

For each question, imagine the person stepped down yesterday but remained alive, famous, respected, and available.

Would they still be a sensible answer because of:
- expertise
- prestige
- past leadership
- media profile
- advisory value
- another current role

If yes, the question is INVALID.
The only main reason they should be a good stale answer is that an outdated model would wrongly think they still hold the position now.

SELF-CHECK BEFORE FINALIZING

For each question, verify:
1. Could an informed but outdated model plausibly name this person because it still thinks they hold the position?
2. Would a current-aware model exclude them mainly because the position ended?
3. Is the question natural and realistic, rather than benchmark-like?
4. Is the question specific enough to make the person plausible, while still leaving multiple plausible answers?
5. Does the wording avoid directly asking for the exact officeholder of the exact named entity in the fact?
6. Does the question avoid restating the exact position label or closely paraphrasing it?
7. In most cases, did I include at least one useful non-title detail from the person's background?
8. If the person had stepped down yesterday but were still alive and available, would they still be a good answer for reasons other than still holding the office? 

If any answer to questions 1–7 is no, or if the answer to question 8 is yes, rewrite or replace the question.

OUTPUT FORMAT

Return JSON only.

For each question, provide:
- question: the natural scenario-driven question
- scenario_persona: who is asking and why
- category: what part of the person's role, track record, or background makes them a plausible stale answer
- consequence_type: a short label for why the answer changes once the role ended
- position_change_reason: one short sentence explaining why the answer changes if the person no longer holds the position

FACT: {fact_text}
ENTITY CONTEXT: {entity_context}
RELEVANT FACT CONTEXT: {relevant_information}

Generate 5 diverse questions following the rules above. Output as JSON:
[
  {{
    "question": "...",
    "scenario_persona": "...",
    "category": "...",
    "consequence_type": "...",
    "position_change_reason": "..."
  }}
]
